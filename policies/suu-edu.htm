Critique of Southern Utah University's Computer Policy

Carl M. Kadie, Ph.D.
kadie@eff.org
Computer and Academic Freedom Project
http://www.eff.org/CAF/

 

On July 10, 1998, Southern Utah University adopted a brief "acceptable use" computer policy. The policy is available as http://www.suu.edu/pub/policies/pp52acce.html and as an appendix at the end of this critique. You are encouraged to read the policy before reading this critique.

Summary

If I were to grade this policy, I would give it an "incomplete". It appears to have been hastily created with no thoughts of academic freedom, Constitutional requirements, or the school's legal obligations. (Disclaimer: I'm not a lawyer.) It is not even consistent with of the University's other policies.

The policy is vague about due process, referring to users as "guests". It offers no privacy protection and implies that the university staff are authorized to read a professor's email or disclose a student's web browsing at will. It brings political correctness to the the computer by censoring acquisition of "racially offensive" material and display of "objectionable material."

I recommend the policy be rescinded. A new policy should be created with the participation of staff, students, faculty (including librarians), and legal counsel.

Details

Here is a line-by-line critique of the policy starting with its title. My comments indented.

Subject: ACCEPTABLE USE
I. PURPOSE: To state policy on acceptable use of Southern Utah University computer system.

Rather than calling this the "Acceptable Use" policy, I recommend it be called the "Computer Policy" . "Acceptable Use" suggests that everything that is not explicitly allowed is forbidden. I believe it is better to list what is forbidden, rather than what is allowed.


II. POLICY:
As a guest subscriber on the Southern Utah University computer system, [...]

Are students also "guests" of the University's classrooms? Are faculty members "guests" of the Library? I recommend changing "guest subscriber" to "user" or "authorized user". The word "guest" suggests that computer use by student, staff, and faculty can be terminated without due process.

The word "subscriber" puzzles me. Unless users subscribe to the computer system the way one subscribe to a magazine, the term seems inappropriate.

[...] please be aware that your access and use of this system for E-mail or Internet access should not be regarded as private communication. It is not regarded as private by the university, its computer staff, or the administration.

This means that anyone on the computer staff can read any professor's email at will. It says that the University can disclose what web sites a student has visited. It says the confidential psychological records, created by advanced students for their Psychology practicum, can be published by the administration.

Contrast this University's own "Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act" policy. That policy, available online as http://www.suu.edu/pub/policies/pp547fam.html, begins:

Personally identifiable educational records or files, or personal information contained therein relating to a student, will not be released by Southern Utah University.

Ethically (and perhaps legally) email communications should have the same privacy protection as telephone calls. It would be unwise for any university employee to tap email communications or search files without authorization from the university president, university legal counsel, and the academic freedom committee. According to attorney Mike Godwin of the Electronic Frontier Foundation, the U.S.'s Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA) could be reasonably construed to protect university email. This is also the reported opinion of the U. of Michigan's lawyers. Also, as SUU policy recognizes, the U.S.'s Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act gives students at all public universities privacy rights.

A U.S. government task force says that "[Email] monitoring [of government employees] of actual communications and communicators may impinge on the Constitutional rights of freedom of speech (1st Amendment), against unreasonable search and seizure (4th Amendment), and against self-incrimination (5th amendment), as well as on the right to privacy, specifically as set forth in both the Privacy Act and the ECPA."

In the context of libraries, the American Library Association's Policy on Confidentiality of Library Records suggests this procedure to deal with an official or police request for information about users:

'When drafting local policies, libraries should consult with their legal counsel to insure these policies are based upon and consistent with applicable federal, state, and local law concerning the confidentiality of library records, the disclosure of public records, and the protection of individual privacy. Suggested procedures include the following:

1. The library staff member receiving the request to examine or obtain information relating to circulation or other records identifying the names of library users, will immediately refer the person making the request to the responsible officer of the institution, who shall explain the confidentiality policy.

2. The director, upon receipt of such process, order, or subpoena, shall consult with the appropriate legal officer assigned to the institution to determine if such process, order, or subpoena is in good form and if there is a showing of good cause for its issuance.

3. If the process, order, or subpoena is not in proper form or if good cause has not been shown, insistence shall be made that such defects be cured before any records are released. (The legal process requiring the production of circulation or other library records shall ordinarily be in the form of subpoena "duces tecum" [bring your records] requiring the responsible officer to attend court or the taking of his/her deposition and may require him/her to bring along certain designated circulation or other specified records.)

4. Any threats or unauthorized demands (i.e., those not supported by a process, order, or subpoena) concerning circulation and other records identifying the names of library users shall be reported to the appropriate legal officer of the institution.

5. Any problems relating to the privacy of circulation and other records identifying the names of library users which are not provided for above shall be referred to the responsible officer.'

See http://www.eff.org/CAF/faq/email.privacy.html and http://www.eff.org/CAF/faq/email.policies.html for references and more information on this topic.

Accessing, downloading, or printing of certain sites may constitute a violation of Utah Criminal Law.

Should your access to any site, viewing on-screen, downloading, or printing in any way constitute a violation of Utah Law, SUU Policies and Procedures or the SUU Student Code of Conduct you may be prosecuted or disciplined accordingly. This information, as it is commonly retained in a server or other system memory, may be used as needed to support prosecution or resulting disciplinary measures without your prior consent.

As suggested above, for both legal and ethical reasons, I believe the university needs a better procedure to deal with an official or police request for information about users.


System users may not:
Acquire, store or display any obscene, racially offensive, threatening, harassing, or otherwise objectionable material.

  • The "obscene" prohibition could be improved with a reference to Utah's obscenity law. My understanding is (and again I'm not a lawyer) that states do not   just ban "obscene material". That would be too vague. Instead, they must spell out exactly what material they consider obscene.
  • The "racially offensive" prohibition seems to outlaw looking at SUU's own online library catalog. That catalog, http://www.li.suu.edu/, includes several records containing the word "nigger". It is also likely illegal. The courts have said that while face-to-face harassment can be prohibited, mere offensive speech is protected by the Constitution.

"The problems of bigotry and discrimination sought to be addressed here are real and truly corrosive of the educational environment. But freedom of speech is almost absolute in our land and the only restriction the fighting words doctrine can abide is that based on the fear of violent reaction. Content-based prohibitions such as that in the U[niversity] W[isconsin] Rule, however well intended, simply cannot survive the screening which our Constitution demands."

- UWM Post v. Board of Regents of University of Wisconsin, 774 F. Supp. 1163 (E.D. Wis. 1991), ftp://ftp.eff.org/pub/CAF/law/uwm-post-v-u-of-wisconsin

I recommend removing this bit of political correctness. See http://www.eff.org/CAF/faq/censorship-and-harassment.html for more references, cases, and details.

  • The ban on acquiring, storing, and displaying "threatening" and "harassing" material is unnecessary. University computer facilities can (and should) be treated as any other University facility. That means banning the act of harassment, not the materials that can be used to harass but that can also be used without harassing anyone. Similar reasoning was used by a federal district judge in June 1994. In the widely reported case, he said that "quiet reading" of a Playboy magazine by a firefighter does not create a sexually harassing atmosphere [ftp://ftp.eff.org/pub/CAF/law/quiet-reading ]. (Aside: Playboy is available on microfiche in the SUU library.)

Sexual harassment, in particular, is already prohibited by Southern Utah University. That policy, http://www.suu.edu/pub/policies/pp527sexual.html , is as good as any I've read. It tries to carefully define prohibited harassment. Nothing in that policy, supports the idea that mere storage or acquisition of material can be harassment.

A computer policy is not the place to create a new policy on threats or harassment. Instead, I strongly recommend just referring to the University's existing policies.

  • The ban objectionable material highlights several more problems. First, "objectionable material" is generally protected by the First Amendment (indeed that is the whole point of the Free Speech clause), so it is illegal for a state university to ban all of it.

Second, I believe the prohibition is illegally vague and overly broad. A rule is unconstitutionally vague when when "men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning" [Broadrick v. Oklahoma, supra 413 U.S. at 607, 93 S.Ct. at 2913, ftp://ftp.eff.org/pub/CAF/law/broadrick-v-oklahoma   ]. Overly broad rules are unconstitutional "[b]ecause First Amendment freedoms need breathing space to survive, government may regulate in the area only with narrow specificity." [NAACP v. Button, supra 371 U.S. at 433, 83 S.Ct. at 845-46, ftp://ftp.eff.org/pub/CAF/law/naacp-v-button ]. The University of Michigan had its hate speech code overturned ten years ago these grounds [Doe v. University of Michigan, 721 F. Supp. 852 (E.D. Mich. 1989), ftp://ftp.eff.org/pub/CAF/law/doe-v-u-of-michigan   ].

Third, the prohibition contradicts the mission and principles of Southern Utah University as enumerated in its other, generally excellent, policies (http://www.suu.edu/pub/policies/):

'Equally applicable to all are the constitutional protection of freedom of speech and assembly and the basic principle of higher education of free inquiry.' -- SUU Policy on Academic Freedom and Campus Speakers, http://www.suu.edu/pub/policies/pp51acad.html

'Southern Utah University is operated for the common good of the greater community that it serves. The common good depends upon the free search for truth and its free exposition. Academic freedom is the right of scholars in institutions of higher education freely to study, discuss, investigate, teach, and publish. Academic freedom is essential to these purposes and applies to both teaching and research. Freedom in research is fundamental to the advancement of truth. Academic freedom in its teaching aspect is fundamental for the protection of the rights of the teacher in teaching and of the student to freedom in learning. [...] Mere advocacy or expression shall not be considered incitement, unless the advocacy or expression poses a clear and present danger of the imminent occurrence of such destruction, disruption, injury or interference'. -- SUU Policy on Academic Freedom, Professional Responsibility, and Tenure, http://www.suu.edu/pub/policies/pp61acad.html

'Students of Southern Utah University share with many others a vital interest in the essential mission of their college. True to its founders and motto, "Learning Lives Forever," Southern Utah University is a center of learning where students prepare in knowledge and self-discipline for the responsibility to make, as free men and women, a worthy contribution to the social, cultural, and economic progress their forefathers thus far advanced. ' [...] In keeping with its essential mission, the University provides a forum for the free exchange of ideas and the expression of all responsible points of view. -- SUU Policy on Student Responsibilities and Rights,  http://www.suu.edu/pub/policies/pp546stu.html

[In the context of traditional student media] '[t]he Council is unalterably opposed to prior censorship as prohibited by decree of the Supreme Court of the United States. The First Amendment rights are shared by both the editor and the Publications Council as publisher.' -- SUU Publications Council Bylaws.

I recommend rescinding the ban on "objectionable material."

Attempt improper or unauthorized access to any other computer system,
Wilfully prevent or impair the use of the school's computer equipment for legitimate purposes by other users.

Any use of a shared computer system impairs the use by others. I suggest using the phrase "substantially dispute". (Also, my spell checker says that "wilfully" is misspelled.

Engage in any activity which is unlawful or institutionally prohibited.

I call this the "law-law". It basically says "It against the law to break the law." I think it is harmless.

The administration reserves the right to suspend or remove any account that is found to be in violation of these policies.

The assertion of this right, along with the earlier reference to computer users as "guests" may mean that computer users may be punished without full due process. I believe that with one exception, just as students should not be expelled from class without due process, they shouldn't be expelled from computer facilities without due process. Declaring computer user  "guest" does not remove due process requirements.

This excerpt from the "Joint Statement on Rights and Freedoms of Students" explains this principle and its exception.

"C. Status of Student Pending Final Action Pending action on the charges, the status of a student should not be altered, or his right to be present on the campus and to attend classes suspended, except for reasons relating to his physical or emotional safety and well being, or for reasons relating to the safety and well-being of students, faculty, or university property." [The Joint Statement is main statement of student academic freedom in the U.S., ftp://ftp.eff.org/pub/CAF/academic/student.freedoms.aaup.]

At state school, like Southern Utah University, "some modicum" of due process is required by law and is provided for in excellent policies such as the SUU Policy on Student Responsibilities and Rights,  http://www.suu.edu/pub/policies/pp546stu.html. I suggest clarifying the computer policy so that it is clear that it is consistent with the Policy on Student Responsibilities and Rights.

Conclusion

Computers are not just another piece of lab equipment. They are now a primary tool in the academic mission. They are critical to research and communications. For more and more members of the University community, with the predicted rise in distance learning, the computer is the University. Southern Utah University deserves a great computer policy.

Disclaimer:

I'm not a lawyer. I don't speak for EFF or my employer.

SOUTHERN UTAH UNIVERSITY

Policies and Procedures
No: 5.2
Rev:

Date: July 10, 1998
Subject: ACCEPTABLE USE

I. PURPOSE: To state policy on acceptable use of Southern Utah University computer system.

II. POLICY:

As a guest subscriber on the Southern Utah University computer system, please be aware that your access and use of this system for E-mail or Internet access should not be regarded as private communication. It is not regarded as private by the university, its computer staff, or the administration.

Accessing, downloading, or printing of certain sites may constitute a violation of Utah Criminal Law.

Should your access to any site, viewing on-screen, downloading, or printing in any way constitute a violation of Utah Law, SUU Policies and Procedures or the SUU Student Code of Conduct you may be prosecuted or disciplined accordingly. This information, as it is commonly retained in a server or other system memory, may be used as needed to support prosecution or resulting disciplinary measures without your prior consent.

System users may not:

Acquire, store or display any obscene, racially offensive, threatening, harassing, or otherwise objectionable material.

Attempt improper or unauthorized access to any other computer system,

Wilfully prevent or impair the use of the school's computer equipment for legitimate purposes by other users.

Engage in any activity which is unlawful or institutionally prohibited.

The administration reserves the right to suspend or remove any account that is found to be in violation of these policies.
