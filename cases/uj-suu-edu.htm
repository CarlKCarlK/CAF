Student falls victim to ACUC policy
Research for class project deemed "objectionable" by student gets soc/psych major booted from lab

By GLENN HALTERMAN
UNIVERSITY JOURNAL EDITOR 

Controversy over SUU's Acceptable Use Policy again arose last week after a student was asked to leave the main computer lab in the ELC in accordance with the eight-month-old policy.

Michaun Jensen, a junior with a psychology/sociology double major from Boulder City, Nev., said that in the late evening Feb. 9 she was doing research on the Internet in the ELC when she was asked to leave.

Jensen said the research was being conducted for a class project for an applied sociology class taught by Dan Pence, associate professor of sociology. The project, she said, dealt with objectionable material with an emphasis on pornography.

Jensen said that she was looking up pornography on the Internet for her paper when, at approximately 11 p.m., a lab proctor, Gary Stewart, told her that she would have to discontinue her search because another student had objected to the images.

Jensen said she stopped looking at pornography and subsequently began to access other sites such as the Ku Klux Klan and sites about Adolf Hitler.

Once again, Jensen said, Stewart approached her and told her that she would have to leave because a student objected to what she was accessing.

Before leaving the lab, Jensen had Stewart sign a paper stating that he was requiring her to go.

Stewart, an SUU student who works in the lab, was unavailable for comment.

Jensen said that she was not upset with Stewart, however, and that she realized that he was just doing his job. Rather, her dissatisfaction was aimed at a computer policy enacted by the university last year.

In the fall, the Acceptable Use Policy adopted by the Academic Computer Users Committee (ACUC) stirred up many on campus who said they felt that it was too vague and potentially unconstitutional. The document had been drafted by an ACUC subcommittee whihc included students

Some 20 students and faculty members approached the ACUC petitioning it to reassess the Acceptable Use policy, a motion which was agreed to in the fall.

However, Jensen and Pence are not yet satisfied.

The current Acceptable Use Policy reads as follows:

As a guest subscriber on the Southern Utah University computer system, please be aware that your access and use of this system for e-mail or Internet access should not be regarded as private communication. It is not regarded as private by the university, its computer staff, or the administration.

Accessing, downloading, or printing of certain sites may constitute a violation of Utah Criminal Law.

Should your access to any site, viewing on-screen, downloading, or printing in any way constitute a violation of Utah Law, SUU Policies and Procedures or the SUU Student Code of Conduct you may be prosecuted or disciplined accordingly. This information, as it is commonly retained in a server or other server memory, may be used as needed to support prosecution or resulting disciplinary measures without your prior consent.

System Users may not:

  • Acquire, store or display any obscene, racially offensive, threatening, harassing or otherwise objectionable material.
  • Attempt improper or unauthorized access to any computer system.
  • Willfully prevent or impair the use of the school¢s computer equipment for legitimate purposes by other users.
  • Engage in any activity which is unlawful or institutionally prohibited.


The administration reserves the right to suspend or remove any account that is found to be in violation of these policies.

"It contradicts their [SUU's] other policies," said Jensen, "like the 'Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act,' SUU's policy of Academic Freedom and Professional Responsibility, the policy on Student Responsibilities and Rights and it goes against my First Amendment rights."

Pence, who spoke in opposition to the policy during the fall ACUC meeting, said that the courts have ruled that objectionable material cannot be legally censored, although obscene material is not protected.

Is there a difference between a picture of pornography and a photo of Hitler?

"Philosophically, no," Pence said. "Politically, yes."

Pence said that it is important to determine the motive of an individual in cases similar to Jensen's.

"My litmus test would be 'What are you going to do with this image?'" he said.

Pence said that Jensen was working on her project to attempt to determine the definition of objectionable in the Acceptable Use policy, which he pointed out deals with more than just sexually objectionable material.

Pence stressed the importance of being sensitive to the people around oneself, especially when dealing sensitive material, saying that 11 p.m. was a more appropriate time of day to do such research rather than at noon when the computer lab is full of other students.

Pence also said that he is uncomfortable with administrators and student proctors serving as a "moral cop."
"That is not the purpose of a university," he said.
