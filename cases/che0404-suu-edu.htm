Utah Student Admits She Contrived Incident to Test Computer-Use Policy

By JEFFREY R. YOUNG

A Southern Utah University student who sparked debate over a controversial computer-use policy now admits that she had asked other students to complain about the pornographic and white-supremacist Web sites she was viewing when she was kicked out of a campus computer laboratory.

"My intention was to test the policy," says the student, Michaun M. Jensen, who is a junior majoring in sociology and psychology. "Someone needed to show the school and the rest of the students what this policy actually meant in practice."

Ms. Jensen acknowledges that she forced the issue by asking other students in the computer lab to complain about her activities. She posted a "confession" on the Internet this week.

Though some have questioned Ms. Jensen's tactics, the incident succeeded in focusing attention on the policy, and also attracted national interest to the issue.

For an applied-sociology course, Ms. Jensen is doing a research project on what kind of material could be banned under the university's acceptable-use policy. The policy, adopted last June, prohibits students from using university computers to "acquire, store, or display any obscene, racially offensive, threatening, harassing, or
otherwise objectionable material." Some students and professors had complained about the policy, arguing that it isoverly broad and infringes the constitutionally protected right to freedom of speech.

One night last month, Ms. Jensen was working on the project in a campus computer lab. She was looking for sites on theWorld-Wide Web that might fall under the policy, she says. While she was viewing a pornographic site, she says, she asked a student near her to report her to the student employee working in the lab.

The employee, Gary Stewart, warned her that if she continued, she could be kicked out of the lab, she says. She then began looking at sites on white supremacy and Hitler, and asked a second student to complain.

Mr. Stewart told The New York Times that the second student had led him to believe that Ms. Jensen wanted to be kicked out of the lab as part of her research project. When he did ask her to leave, he believed he was doing her a favor, the Times reported. Mr. Stewart could not be reached for comment yesterday.

Ms. Jensen, however, says that she did not expect to be kicked out of the lab, and that she did not ask to be removed. She says that having asked students to complain about her did not weaken her message. "My point is that it could have been reported either way," she says. "This is a really conservative school, and there are people" who would object to such material.

The chairman of the committee that created the policy, David J. Whiting, says he agrees that the policy needs to be reviewed "regardless of the propriety of the student's actions." Mr. Whiting is an assistant professor of teacher education. He adds that the committee, known as the Academic Computer Users Committee, is scheduled to review the issue anyway at a meeting Thursday.

Mr. Whiting says he agrees that the policy is overly vague. "The word 'objectionable' shouldn't have been there, there's no doubt about that." He says it is difficult, in crafting computer-use policies, to "balance the university's liability and the need for a fair and appropriate environment with academic freedom."

The original policy, he adds, "reflects the best efforts of the people who put it together."
