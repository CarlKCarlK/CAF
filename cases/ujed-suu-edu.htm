COMPUTER POLICY MUST BE CHANGED

As is common among members of humankind, good people with good intentions
often do unwise things to further their honorable aims. With no malicious
intent, decisions can be made that have unwanted and unforeseen
consequences.

Such is the case with those collective few who drafted and subsequently
ratified the university's current Acceptable Use Policy dealing with campus
computers.

The Journal does not doubt the motive of the ACUC and the purpose of the
policy to make individuals aware that there are laws dealing with what can
and cannot legally be accessed by campus computers, but does feel, as so
many others do, that the vagueness of the policy poses a problem.

But now, in light of events recently reported in the Journal, proponents
of the Acceptable Use Policy are thrust into an unenviable and uncomfortable
position. They have the choice to (a) deem Adolf Hitler, arguably the most
important historical figure of the 20th century, as "objectionable"
according to the policy and off-limits for Internet access or (b) admit the
validity of the arguments of the policy's detractors regarding the problems
of the policy which stem from its inherent vagueness, particularly in the
definition of "objectionable" material.

It has to be one or the other, and in legal terms, this is a prima facia
case, meaning that the facts in this situation are obvious enough as to
render argument on the matter unnecessary.

The Journal trusts those involved will pursue an honorable course,
providing the student body with justification for subsequent actions which
are surely shortly forthcoming.

Meanwhile, the ACUC took the first step to remedy the problematic policy
when it voted in early November to reassess the university's current course
of cyber censorship. Thus far, how no tangible fruits of the subcommittee
set up to review the policy have been seen. The Journal has recently been
informed that the subcommittee has only met once (in November). We must ask,
"What, exactly, is the hold up?"

Furthermore, the Journal acknowledges the destructive and corrosive
nature of obscene pornographic materials and agrees with past Supreme Court
rulings which do not give protection to such speech. SUU, the Journal
submits, is within its rights to limit access to such material.

However, are we to also now assume that child abuse, partial birth
abortion, prostitution, female genital mutilation and topics of a similar
vein, though decidedly important in today's societal dialogue, are
sufficiently objectionable as to be banned from SUU's Internet system?

If not, let's get the policy fixed to make it clear that such sensitive
subjects are not taboo.

If so, it's probably time to call the ACLU.
