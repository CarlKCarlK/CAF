Thursday, February 25, 1999

Southern Utah University's Internet-Use Policy Is Under Debate

By JEFFREY R. YOUNG

Students and faculty members at Southern Utah University are
debating a controversial computer-use policy under which a
student was kicked out of a campus computer lab this month for
looking at Web sites devoted to pornography and Hitler.

The student, Michaun Jensen, was using the computer lab to do
research for a class project on what kinds of material would fall
under the university's acceptable-use policy, according
to an account in the student newspaper, The University
Journal.

Another student in the public computer room who saw the
material on Ms. Jensen's monitor complained to Gary Stewart, a
student employee working in the lab. Mr. Stewart first warned
Ms. Jensen to avoid such pages and then asked her to leave, citing
the university's policy. The policy prohibits students from using
university computers to "acquire, store, or display any obscene,
racially offensive, threatening, harassing, or otherwise
objectionable material."

Neither Mr. Stewart nor Ms. Jensen could be reached for
comment.

The incident has revived campus discussion of the computer-use
policy, which the university adopted in June. Some professors and
students say the policy is overly broad and infringes the
constitutionally protected right to freedom of speech.

Last weekend, someone placed blindfolds -- scrawled with the
message "see no evil" -- on a dozen statues of great thinkers on
the campus, and signs appeared protesting the computer-use
policy.

Neal L. Cox, a spokesman for the university, says the policy is
meant to insure that campus computer labs -- which are often
crowded -- are not used for non-academic purposes, and that
students don't use the machines in ways that disrupt the work of
their classmates. "We're not running a game room or a
peepshow," he says.

He says the policy was modeled on computer-use policies at other
universities, such as Idaho State University and the University of
Oregon.

Mr. Cox says that university officials are looking into Ms. Jensen's
ejection, and that some administrators wonder whether she even
violated the policy, since she says she was doing research when
she was told to leave.

In a November 5 article about the policy in The University
Journal, Michael Carter, a Utah assistant attorney general who
serves as legal counsel for the university, said: "If someone has a
legitimate educational purpose [for accessing a particular site], that
should be all right."

After earlier campus discussion of the policy, the university formed
a subcommittee to review it. But the subcommittee met only once
and disbanded without altering the policy, Mr. Cox says.

The committee that created the policy, the Academic Computer
Users Committee, is scheduled to meet next week, and it may
review the policy, Mr. Cox says. "The recent incident in the
computer lab has certainly refocused attention to review the
policy," he adds.
