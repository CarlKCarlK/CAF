Critique of the Lewis/Barnes Policy Proposal

Southern Utah University is considering several alternatives to their current computer policy. This is a critique of the proposal of Professors Timothy B. Lewis and Jeffrey N. Barnes. Their original is at http://www.business.suu.edu/business/fac/lewis_t/computerpolicy.htm (Also Lewis-Barnes Alternative Draft). The professors make additional comments at http://www.business.suu.edu/business/fac/lewis_t/internetpornography.htm.

Two good things about the proposal. First, its authors are honest about their motivations. Some would-be censors claim to be motivated primarily by a desire to enforce federal equal rights laws. This claim often rings hollow. Lewis and Barnes are more up front with their motivation: they want to save civilization from the "anarchy and violence [that] would ultimately reign" from, for example, lewd descriptions of nipples. In this spirit, their proposal considers all matters sexual but drops the current policy's controversial ban on racially "objectionable" material.

Second, less honest would-be censors would avoid legal arguments, because weak legal arguments can be challenged. Lewis and Barnes, in contrast, bravely put forward the best legal argument they can. For example, they say their proposal is legal because a federal law excepts the University from constitutional requirements. In support of this amazing claim that a simple statute can trump the Constitution, they courageously provide an authority: Bruce Taylor of the National Law Center for Children and Families. Recall that Mr. Taylor was the architect of the Communications Decency Act (CDA) and that he predicted that the CDA would pass constitutional muster. Also recall that the Supreme Court, the most conservative Court in my lifetime, rejected his CDA, 7-0.

My greatest fear is not that Lewis/Barnes proposal will be accepted, but rather that it will lead to a let's-only-burn-half-the-books "compromise". I hope to show in the rest of this critique that the proposal and the legal arguments that support it are bankrupt and should be given no weight.

(A disclaimer: Unlike Dr. Lewis, my doctorate is in computer science, not the law. I'll do my best.)

The proposal starts: "No individual right or liberty has ever been deemed to be absolute[.]". This may be a  effort to brand all who would oppose the proposal as absolutists who will bring "anarchy and violence" to civilization. In defense of those who oppose the policy,  none that I know of are absolutist. Also, as far as I know, Snow College in Utah is the only public college to "filter" student access to the Internet (as this proposal recommends). I have not noticed the predicted dire effects at our Nation's other schools. Indeed, schools with what I would consider good computer policies, seem to be doing fine.

The proposal then calls for "balance". For Lewis and Barnes, "balance" requires Southern Utah University to:

  1. Be the first public university, to my knowledge, to "filter" students.
  2. Challenge well-established First Amendment law, for example, the Public Forum Doctrine.
  3. Challenge well-established 14th Amendment law that binds  SUU to the U.S. Constitution.

I have a counter-proposal to "balance" academic freedom and First Amendment rights with the right to free illegal sexual harassment: Enforce a well-crafted, general sexual harassment policy. SUU already has such a general sexual harassment policy. This would treat computer resources and media with the same respect as other campus resources and media.

The preamble of the Lewis and Barnes proposal continues: "[T]here are legitimate boundaries imposed by the greater community that we serve and whose financial support is necessary to the accomplishment of our academic mission. ... [T]he moral boundaries imposed upon us are determined by our surrounding community." and later:   "...[t]he university is free to discriminate regarding what information and ideas it will institutionally sponsor and promote through its public facilities. Such decisions are a matter of sponsorship rather than censorship ...".

Or, to paraphrase: since freedom of the press belongs to those who own presses, a public university can do anything it wants with the media and facilities that it owns, right? No. Like any organization, the government agencies, including public universities, must work within their charter (the Constitution). The U.S. Supreme Court has said that this limits the Government's authority to control the media that owns and controls. The rationale is that it would be dangerous for a Government that is elected by the people to have too much control on what the people can say and read.

The Supreme Court calls created forums, like a student newspaper or campus mail systems, limited public forums. It says that the government can limited who may access these forums and/or what topics may be discussed. But otherwise, "it is bound by the same standards as apply in a traditional public forum"; "content-based prohibition must be narrowly drawn to effectuate a compelling state interest." For example, viewpoint-based discrimination is forbidden.

http://www.eff.org/ftp/pub/CAF/law/rosenberger_v_u_virginia   - A 1995 U.S. Supreme Court decision that confirmed that the
state universities cannot discriminate on the basis of viewpoint in (government-owned) limited public forums.
http://www.eff.org/ftp/pub/CAF/law/san-diego-committee-v-gov-bd   - A decision by an appellate court that applied the Supreme Court's Public
Forum Doctrine (to a school newspaper).
http://www.eff.org/ftp/pub/CAF/law/stanley-v-magrath - Comments from Public Schools Law: Teachers' and Students' Rights 2nd Ed. by Martha M. McCarthy and Nelda H. Cambron-McCabe It says, in part, "the content of a school-sponsored paper that is established as a medium for student expression cannot be regulated more closely than a nonsponsored paper".
http://www.eff.org/ftp/pub/CAF/law/constraints.constitutional   - Comments from A Practical Guide to Legal Issues Affecting College
Teachers by Partrica A. Hollander, D. Parker Young, and Donald D. Gehring. Discusses the constitutional constraints on public universities including the requires for freedom of expression, freedom against unreasonable searches and seizures, due process, specific rules.
http://www.eff.org/ftp/pub/CAF/law/rust-v-sullivan - The Supreme Court's abortion-gag-rule decisions. The decision explicitly mentions universities as a place where free expression is so important that gag rules would not be allowed.

 

The proposal says that computer users have a right to "protection of public morality and decency." I'm not sure, but think it may means a right not to be offended. If so, then I believe the proposal is mistaken. One responsibility we must accept as the price of living in a free country is that we will be offended. In the famous Cohen v. California case, the Supreme Court said a man couldn't be punished for the offensive words on his jacket. [ftp://ftp.eff.org/pub/CAF/law/cohen-v-california.1]. In Papish v. University of Missouri, the Supreme Court ordered the reinstatement of a university student expelled for distributing an underground newspaper sporting the headline "Motherfucker acquitted" on the grounds that "the mere dissemination of ideas--no matter how offensive to good taste--on a state university campus may not be shut off in the name alone of conventions of decency." [ftp://ftp.eff.org/pub/CAF/law/papish-v-u-of-missouri]. In 1994 case, a federal judge said that quiet reading of Playboy by a firefighter did not create an illegally harassing environment. [ftp://ftp.eff.org/pub/CAF/law/quiet-reading].

The proposal requires among other things "Respect for the moral and intellectual beliefs of others" and "Civility". I believe these requirements to be unfairly and unconstitutionally vague and overly broad. [See http://www.eff.org/CAF/policies/suu-edu.htm for more on clarity requirements.]

The proposal bans any access to sexually explicit material unless three specified University officials approve. This section seems to be based on a Virginia law that is still being challenged in the courts. That law banned state employees from looking at sexual explicit material on the job via computer. It does not censor students. Nothing in the law or the court's decision to-date supports censoring students. http://www.aclu.org/news/1999/n021099a.html.

With respect to censoring university students, a much more germane Virginia case is Mainstream Loudoun v. Board of Trustees, http://www.aclu.org/news/n112398a.html. In that case, the judge ruled said that forcing adults to use blocking software in public libraries "offends the guarantee of free speech," and permanently blocked government officials in Loudoun County, Virginia from unconstitutionally restricting online access. She rejected the notion that access to the Internet "should really be construed as a library acquisition decision, to which the First Amendment does not apply, rather than a decision to remove library materials."

(I would also add I find that the academic freedom implications needing such approval to do research is ghastly)

The proposal's definition of sexually explicit is much, much broader than "obscenity". It bans, among other things "any description of  ... sexual conduct". It also really does ban descriptions (and pictures) of lewd exhibitions of nipples. And also, "descriptions of flagellation ... by .. a person who is ... clad in ... bizarre costume."

This, of course, covers much of literature, much of art, many religious books, and all Zorro movies. And what is the proposed penalty, after due process, for accessing safe-sex pages, descriptions of lewd nipples, and descriptions of flagellation  by a person who is clad in bizarre costume? One year expulsion from the computer system. For many students, this would be equivalent to a one year expulsion from the school. The penalty for a 2nd look at safe-sex pages? Expulsion from school or termination of employment.

(In general, I think it is bad policy to specify a specific punishment for a university offensive. And, of course, these punishments are overly harsh.)

The proposal ends with a call for "filtering software". Yesterday, the Censorware Project reported on the "filtering software" used in some Utah libraries. That report, http://censorware.net/reports/utah/, found that when a user was banned  from a site by the State's Internet "filtering" software, more likely than not, the site was completely innocent. Among the documents banned by Utah: the Holy Bible, the Book of  Mormon, the Declaration of Independence, the United States
Constitution, all of Shakespeare's plays, and The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes. Note these are not hypotheticals. Real citizens of Utah really tried to read this material and the State really stopped them.

A university is no place for such filters. (I also don't see how letting a company in California decide what academics can see supports community rights.)

Here are some more or less randomly ordered comments:

In their supporting notes, Lewis and Barnes say:

a city was constitutionally allowed to prohibit the posting of political campaign literature on city property even in the face of free speech challenges since there were other avenues of expression open to the candidates. Closing off one avenue of expression did not foreclose all others. (Members of City Council of Los Angeles v. Taxpayers for Vincent, 466 U.S. 789 (1984)

I find this a strange summary of the case. I would say that the Court decided that public telephone poles weren't forums and so speech didn't have to be allowed there. Anyone can read http://caselaw.findlaw.com/cgi-bin/getcase.pl?court=US&vol=466&invol=789 and see for themselves. (The Court in that case also wrote "The First Amendment forbids the government to regulate speech in ways that favor some viewpoints or ideas at the expense of others.") The Chicago Park District did try to employ reasoning like that suggested by Lewis and Barnes. The courts rejected their attempt to create free speech ghettos in Collin v. Chicago Park District 460 F2d 746 (7th Cir, 1972).

Lewis Barnes summarize NEA vs. Finley with "Thus, the NEA was constitutionally free to reject materials that it deemed to be indecent.". If you read the Court's official summary, however, you'll see:

The provision, however, simply adds "considerations" to the grant-making process; it does not preclude awards to projects that might be deemed "indecent" or "disrespectful," nor place conditions on grants, or even specify that those factors must be given any particular weight in reviewing an application. - http://caselaw.findlaw.com/cgi-bin/getcase.pl?court=US&vol=000&invol=97-371

Also, in that case the Court references a case that seems more on-topic: Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 837: 'in which the Court overturned a public university's objective decision denying funding to all student publications having religious editorial viewpoints-is therefore misplaced. The NEA's mandate is to make aesthetic judgments, and the inherently content-based "excellence" threshold for NEA support sets it apart from the subsidy at issue in Rosenberger.' (from the official summary.)

One of the important cases striking down Campus Speech Codes was UWM v. U. of Wisconsin That federal court case dismissed "fighting words arguments" similar to those of Lewis and Barnes. It also covered the difference between university employees and students, and the topic trying to trump a the Constitution with a statue. Here is a quote:

However, its argument regarding Title VII law has at least three difficulties. First, Title VII addresses employment, not educational, settings. Second, even if Title VII governed educational settings, the Meritor holding would not apply to this case. The Meritor Court held that courts should look to agency principles when determining whether an employer is to be held liable for its employee's actions. See id. Since employees may act as their employer's agents, agency law may hold an employer liable for its employees actions. In contrast, agency theory would generally not hold a school liable for its students' actions since students normally are not agents of the school. Finally, even if the legal duties set forth in Meritor applied to this case, they would not make the UW Rule constitutional. Since Title VII is only a statute, it cannot supersede the requirements of the First Amendment.
[ftp://ftp.eff.org/pub/CAF/law/uwm-post-v-u-of-wisconsin]

This proposal doesn't address other aspects of the current computer policy, for example, privacy protection.

Here quotes and paraphrases from comments about the proposal from a law and computers mailing list:

The "supporting document" written by Professor Lewis is simply an opinion piece.  It is primarily comprised of literary and political quotations that have no bearing on the any legal analysis of the proposed policy. It reflects one individual's view of what the law should be.  He is entitled to that view, but his analysis vis a vis current law is completely off-base.  Indeed, he systematically misrepresents current law, as for example in equating the display of sexually graphic materials with the Chaplinsky "fighting words" standard.  His claim that the Reno decision does not apply to the SUU situation is flatly wrong as a matter of legal analysis.   Similarly, his citation of 47 U.S.C. sec. 230 (c)(2) is grossly inappropriate, if not disingenuous.  Nearly all the other citations Lewis makes to legal authority are mistaken or inapposite as well.

As to the proposed policy itself, it is badly flawed, and almost certainly unconstitutional on its face.  The policy is clearly not content-neutral -- it is content specific.  It is not clear that it serves any compelling governmental interest -- although there is reference to "protecting minors," this interest would be greatly diminished by the fact that any minors who might access material via SUU are probably in their late teens, and would presumably be doing so in a university setting.

Assuming that there is some compelling interest, the policy is neither narrowly tailored, nor does it adopt the least restrictive means.  It clearly reaches protected speech, and protected speech beyond what is necessary to achieve any legitimate purpose.  The requirement that a waiver, signed in triplicate, be sought before a student or faculty member is allowed to access protected expression is certainly more than unduly burdensome.

Additionally, the use of filterware by a state actor is blatant content-specific censorship, and can easily be shown not to be narrowly tailored or a least restrictive means.

Finally, I note that the policy is simply wrong in claiming that the standard for expression is dictated by the surrounding community: the constitution imposes a national standard in every case (including Miller obscenity).  In his commentary, Lewis claims that those who disagree with currently accepted standards should be free to exit and found their own community where standards differ; this is surely correct with regard to his desire to live in a community that suppresses protected speech, but the community that desired to do so would need to be somewhere outside of the United States, its territories and posessions.
