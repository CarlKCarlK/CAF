I'll say that I think Karl goes too far in seriously talking about jail
terms etc. here.   The fact is nobody is going to jail over this.

I would rather talk about likely outcomes, which are much milder but
still scary.   The likely event that scares me is that Playboy or
Paramount or Penthouse or some other big copyright holder decides to
do a "crackdown" on USENET.

This "crackdown" isn't even lawsuits, unless we have some recalcitrant
admins.  It's just lawyer's letters.  Lawyer's letters to the admins of
systems where pirate posters lived, letters to admins of major sites that
carry the group (easy to spot from Path lines) and letters to feeder sites
like UUNET.

I think the letters would be effective.  Most people would do what they
said.  All the major corporations and universities would.  They take a
"who needs trouble?" attitude, and since copyright violation is not a free
speech issue, they won't see a downside to cutting off the groups entirely,
and probably a whole bunch more with them.   "Who needs trouble?"

Some smaller sites and BBSs will keep the groups.  They will get more
stern warnings, some may even get lawsuits.  All these will be settled
out of court, loosely in favour of the copyright holders.

This, of course, is what some people seem to be calling for.  They say
that Playbody should be the one to make this stop, not the people of
the community of USENET.

But let's consider the above, entirely probable situation, and how it
looks.   If it reaches the press, it looks very bad for USENET.
USENET gets a rap as a pirate network that needs to be shut down by
lawyer's letters.   The outside public won't know the difference between
"alt" and "comp."  They won't doubt that the piracy was there.

The higher-ups at sites all over will get a new perception of the net,
and a lowered opinion about it.

This is sort of your best-case scenario.   Worse cases include:
	a) Somebody fights hard, and loses
	b) Somebody fights hard, and wins on a technicality
		(Yes, this is a bad scenario.  To the outside world
		the pirate label will stick, and the higher-ups will
		still see the cost of the suits.  Rarely does the press
		cover who wins nearly as much as they cover the
		allegations.)
	c) The copyright holders decide to sue deep pocket sites for
		money, as a revenue generator, rather than just warn
		them off.

That somebody could fight and win decisively seems almost impossible to me,
based on my judgements and strong knowledge of the net.

What "Fair Use" is 
Newsgroups: alt.comp.acad-freedom.talk,alt.binaries.pictures.erotica.d,news.admin.misc
From: brad@clarinet.com (Brad Templeton)
Subject:  What "Fair Use" is
Date: Mon, 27 Dec 1993 05:07:56 GMT
Message-ID: <1993Dec27.050756.8888@clarinet.com>

>These prohibitions are irrelevant (unless they are part of a valid
>contract.) The copyright owner does not define fair use. (It it did,
>there likely would be no fair use). Fair use is defined by statute,
>rule, and case law.

And tops the list of things widely misunderstood on USENET.  While I
am not a lawyer, I make my living via copyright law, so I will offer
the following explanation for folks.   Perhaps some day a copyright
FAQ will be assembled with a later version of this.

Copyright law (which, in general, grants to the creator the right to
permit or deny almost all forms of copying of a work or the creation of
derivative works) has in it a special exception known as "fair use."

Sometimes, one may copy a portion of a copyrighted work if the purpose
of that copying is one of the purposes the legislature wanted to see
unhindered.  In general, the authors of copyright law felt that applied too
far, it might be a burden on free speech *about* copyrighted works,
namely criticism of them, reporting news about them or educating students
about them.

The fair use exemption allows you to make a copy of a small portion of
a copyrighted work, for a limited number of purposes, most notably those
above.   This may be done without -- or indeed against -- the permission
of the copyright holder.  In general, you must do both:
	a) Reproduce only the necessary excerpt from the work
	b) Have the right purpose

What "the right purpose" is is defined to some extent in case law.
But the most common are:

	a) Criticism (including parody and satire)
	b) News reporting
	c) Education

These are often misunderstood, particularly (b).  It is not, for example,
legal for one person reporting the news to simply copy the words of
another reporter.   You can reproduce sections of a copyrighted work in
reporting the news if the subject of your news report is the copyrighted
work.   Ie. if you are writing a story about Jimmy Carter's new book, you
can quote from it.  "The President said, 'blah blah blah.'"  In this case
the news is that Carter wrote something, not the thing that he wrote about.
Posting entire newspaper articles to USENET is thus not normally fair use.

(Note however that individual facts can't be copyrighted.  You can always
rewrite a news story in your own words, though it is normal to attribute
your source.)

If you detect a theme here, it's that a "fair use" is not an attempt to
exploit the value of a copyrighted work, but an attempt to talk about the
work *AS A WORK*.   This is a gray area, though, because there have been
parodies, for example, that have also exploited their subject for benefit,
and such a case is currently before the U.S. Supreme Court (The 2 Live Crew's
version of "Oh, Pretty Woman.")

But note that money is only peripheral.  Just because you don't charge money
does not mean it is fair use, and just because you do charge does not
mean it is not fair use.  In fact, most fair uses, such as reviews in
the newspaper, are charged for.

In general, you can't include an entire, stand-alone work, unless it's
part of a much larger work.   A clip with 1,000 frames from a movie can
be fair use in a movie review.  A single still posted to USENET without
comment is not fair use.

If the work itself is very short, just a few paragraphs, then it's a gray
area.  In one extreme case, a 2-stanza poem, quoted in its entirety, was
ruled to not be fair use, poems having the compact nature that they do.

It's also generally necessary that you attribute the work to its author
and/or owner.  If you're unable or unwilling to attribute, it pretty much
is not fair use.

When reposting an excerpt, as yourself:

	a) Is this just enough to get my point through?
	b) Why am I posting this?  Is it to make a point, or just because
	   I am too laze to redo it in my own words?
	c) Did I attribute the source?
	d) Am I writing about the work, or am I just publishing the work?

It's not defined in the law, but attempts at criticism or review do need
to be legitimate.  Taking a work and putting a head on it of, "Hey, check
this out, I liked it" is unlikely to be considered a critical essay.  If
the included work is significantly longer than your comments, that's a
bad sign.

In general, most USENET "included quotes" probably are fair use, though no
court has ruled on it.  They fit very well with the purpose of the fair
use exemption.  You are holding a debate with the author, and reminding
the audience of the author's words.

The legislators realized that without the fair use exemption, nobody could
properly criticise or parody copyrighted works, for they would rarely
get permission.  This is the purpose of the exemption.  Are you following
that purpose?  If not, you probably aren't making a fair use.

Copyright 1993 by UPI. Reposted with permission from the ClariNet

Date: Tue, 18 May 93 19:07:23 PDT

	NEW YORK (UPI) -- A federal jury Tuesday found that $400,000 in civil
damages was due controversial professor Leonard Jeffries who was
stripped last year of his chairmanship of the black studies department
at the City University of New York.
	Last week, the same jury found the school had discriminated against
Jeffries when it ousted him in March 1992, saying a speech he gave in
1991 was a ``motivating factor'' in CUNY's decision to deny him
reappointment as chairman, an apparent violation of his right to free
speech.
	In a speech made in Albany on July 20, 1991, Jeffries asserted that
Jews helped finance the slave trade and that Jews and Italians in
Hollywood conspired to disparage blacks in film.
	Excerpts of the speech were published in the New York Post and set
off demands for Jeffries' ouster.
	City College officials said Jeffries was removed as chairman in March
1992 because the furor of criticism that followed his speech made him an
ineffective department head.
	In charging CUNY with violating his First Amendment rights, Jeffries
sought $25 million in damages and reinstatement as chairman, a position
he held for almost 20 years.
	The decision whether to reinstate Jeffries as chairman and whether to
award the amount the jury seeks will be made by Judge Kenneth Conboy,
said a spokesman for state Attorney General Robert Abrams, whose office
represented CUNY officials in the suit.
	``A jury finding is just that, a finding,'' said the spokesman,
Edward Barbini. ``It's up to the judge to either agree to (the $400,000
award) or set it aside.''
	Barbini added that CUNY has indicated it will appeal the ruling.
	The jury found that CUNY based their decision to strip Jeffries of
his title as chairman on the ``reasonable expectation'' that he should
have known that his speech would disrupt the operation of his
department.
	But the court held that was not enough, that the school should have
more definitely proved Jeffries was an ineffective leader, regardless of
the speech.

censorship policy, including my own, Netcom.  I take it that Mr. White does
not like to see "Fanny Hill" available anywhere on the Internet, otherwise he
would not have gone to the trouble of posting as he did (nor used the
phrase "unsuitable upload" in the title of his post).  People post based on
what's important to them and I guess censorship of "Fanny Hill" is important
to Mr. White for what he feels are good reasons.  Though I'm sure his "good
reasons" may have *some* merit on the surface, I also believe he is being a
little short-sighted as to the bigger picture -- and censorship of any kind
is *never* to be taken lightly.

After all, the tables could get turned someday and I'm sure Mr. White would
not want his ideas/views/writings to be censored because someone else labels
them 'dangerous'.  For example, many on the conservative Christian Right have
been in the forefront in the past of trying to censor "Fanny Hill".  However,
it is a frightening possibility that in the future, a despotic government,
based on atheism, secular humanism, or some other religion, may instead try
to suppress the views and writings of the Christian Right, labeling them as
"dangerous" for an "enlightened society", and unsuitable to teach our children
(and if you don't think this could happen, think again!).  Thus, it is
extremely short-sighted for the Christian Right to attempt any censorship
since the precedents set now could be used against them later!  I urge Mr.
White to reconsider his attempt at censoring "Fanny Hill" on the Internet,
no matter what his reasons are.

Now, about cica, et al.  My first statement is that if the cica and wuarchive
administrators feel that it is not appropriate for their site, I won't argue
any more past this post, since they will do what they will do.  I hope that
they do carefully consider the long-term ramifications of their actions and
not succumb to short-term vision and the aggressiveness of a small but vocal
minority.

And let me give a couple more reasons that the site administrators should
consider:

First of all, "Fanny Hill" was declared "not obscene" by the U.S. Supreme
Court back in the more repressive climate of 1966.  My local public library
has a copy on the shelves, and I asked them yesterday about their policy of
loaning it out to minors -- they said that any book on their shelves can be
loaned out by minors!  So, if a public library in the still-repressive U.S.
can loan "Fanny Hill" out to minors, what does that say about the distribution
of "Fanny Hill" electronically?

"Fanny Hill" is also considered by many scholars to be one of the great works
of English literature;  it's not like most erotica today which barely qualifies
as even literary.

I urge the administrators at cica and wuarchive to take into consideration
the special nature of "Fanny Hill" as one of the great works of literature
in the English language.  It goes beyond the usual definition of "adult
material".  The usually simplistic rule of "prohibiting adult material" does
not take into account special circumstances, and "Fanny Hill" is a definite
*special* circumstance.  Please keep it available for downloading just as any
U.S. public library keeps it on its shelves for use by all!
